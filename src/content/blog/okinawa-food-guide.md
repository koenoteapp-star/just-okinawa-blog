Okinawan food is not Japanese food.

It shares some ingredients, some techniques, and some influences — but it arrived here by a different route. Okinawa was an independent kingdom for centuries, trading with China, Southeast Asia, and the rest of Japan on its own terms. The food reflects that.

This is a guide to what you should eat in Okinawa, why it tastes the way it does, and how to find the real version of it.

---

## The Essentials

### Okinawa Soba (沖縄そば)

The dish most associated with Okinawa, and the one most visitors get wrong on the first try.

Okinawa soba is not soba. The noodles are thick wheat noodles — closer to udon than the buckwheat noodles the name suggests — served in a clear broth made from pork bones and dried bonito. The toppings are typically slow-braised pork ribs (soki そーき) or belly pork (rafute ラフテー), pickled ginger, and spring onion.

The broth is lighter than ramen, the pork is richer than you expect, and a good bowl of soba in a small local shop will cost you around ¥700–900.

**Where to find it:** Everywhere. But the best bowls are in small, undecorated shops in residential neighbourhoods — not the tourist-facing restaurants around Kokusai Street.

---

### Champuru (チャンプルー)

The word means "mixed together" in the Okinawan dialect, and that's exactly what champuru is — a stir-fry of whatever needs using up.

The most famous version is **goya champuru**: bitter melon (ゴーヤー), tofu, egg, and pork, stir-fried together with simple seasoning. The bitter melon is the defining flavour — sharp, green, and genuinely bitter in a way that grows on you.

Other versions use fu (wheat gluten), papaya, or tofu alone. All of them are fast, cheap, and impossible to find in the same form outside of Okinawa.

---

### Rafute (ラフテー)

Pork belly, braised for hours in awamori (Okinawan rice spirit), soy sauce, and sugar until it collapses when you touch it. The fat becomes translucent and soft; the meat below it falls apart.

Rafute is Okinawan comfort food at its most honest. It appears in champuru, on top of soba, or on its own as a side dish. Order it wherever you see it.

---

### Taco Rice (タコライス)

A local invention with an unlikely origin. American military bases in Okinawa in the 1980s inspired a dish that has since become a local institution: seasoned ground beef, cheese, lettuce, tomato, and salsa served over white rice.

It sounds like a shortcut. It tastes better than it has any right to. Every local has a favourite place to get it.

---

### Jimami Tofu (ジーマーミ豆腐)

Made from peanuts rather than soybeans, jimami tofu has a dense, slightly sweet texture that sits somewhere between regular tofu and a firm pudding. It's usually served chilled, with a light sauce.

Subtle and easy to overlook. Worth seeking out.

---

### Sata Andagi (サーターアンダーギー)

Okinawan doughnuts — dense, slightly sweet, fried until the outside is dark and the inside is soft. They're sold at markets, festivals, and roadside stalls across the island.

Eat them warm. They're nothing special cold.

## What to Drink

### Awamori (泡盛)

Okinawa's own distilled spirit, made from long-grain rice using a black koji mould. The result is drier and stronger than sake — typically 25–43% ABV — with a flavour that varies from sharp and clean to earthy and complex depending on age.

Old awamori (古酒 / kūsu) that has been aged for three years or more is the version worth seeking. Drink it on the rocks or with a little water.

### Orion Beer

The local beer, brewed in Naha since 1957. Light, clean, and cold — exactly what you want when it's 32°C and you've just come off the beach. Order it everywhere.

## Where to Eat in Naha

### Kokusai Street Area

Okinawa's main tourist street has restaurants, but most of them are designed for visitors. Useful for a first meal; not where the locals eat.

### Makishi Public Market (牧志公設市場)

The first floor sells fresh fish, meat, and vegetables — including the purple yam (imo), bitter melon, and sea grapes (うみぶどう) that define Okinawan cooking. The second floor has small restaurants that will cook what you buy downstairs.

Go in the morning when it's busy. This is what a market looks like when it's for the people who live here.

### Nishizaki-cho and Tsuboya

Quieter neighbourhoods with local soba shops and small izakayas. No English signage. Exactly the kind of place where the food is good because it has to be.

## A Day of Eating in Okinawa

Okinawan food rewards the people who graze rather than plan. A day of eating here might look like this:

- **Morning:** Soba at a local shop, open from 7am
- **Lunch:** Champuru set at a neighbourhood shokudo (定食屋)
- **Afternoon:** Sata andagi from a market stall
- **Evening:** Rafute and awamori at a small izakaya

None of it needs a reservation. Most of it costs less than you'd spend at a chain restaurant at home.

## A Few Things Worth Knowing

- **Pork is everywhere.** Okinawan cuisine uses every part of the pig. If you don't eat pork, ask carefully — it appears in broths and seasonings as well as in obvious dishes.
- **Portions are generous.** A set meal (定食) typically comes with rice, miso soup, and a side dish. You won't leave hungry.
- **Prices are reasonable.** A proper meal at a local restaurant costs ¥700–1,500. Tourist-facing restaurants charge more for less.

---

The food in Okinawa is worth paying attention to.

It tells the story of the islands — where they came from, what they traded, who they were before they became part of Japan. A bowl of soba and a glass of awamori is a better introduction to Okinawa than any museum.
